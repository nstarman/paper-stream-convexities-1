Most of a galaxy's mass is dark matter, and what dark matter is sets the shape of the halo it forms.
If dark matter is cold and collisionless (CDM), halos are triaxial: squashed differently along each
of three axes. If its particles scatter off one another (self-interacting dark matter, SIDM), the
collisions can round halos to be more spherical.

Stellar streams trace a halo's shape. A stellar stream forms when the host galaxy's tides pull
apart a satellite galaxy or globular cluster; its stars spread out along nearly the same orbit. In
a spherical halo, a stream observed by an external view always appears to bend toward the centre
of its galaxy. In a triaxial halo it need not: a segment of it can bend away. We call such a
segment **convex**. Finding one needs only the stream's path on the sky and
where its galaxy's centre is: no velocities, no distances, and no model of the stream or the halo.

How often streams show a convex segment therefore follows how triaxial halos are. In our
simulations, streams in CDM halos have a convex segment longer than <Var name="cosmology/ell_min" />
about twice as often as streams in rounder SIDM halos. With enough streams, counting the convex
ones tells the two apart.

Today's catalogs hold about a hundred streams beyond the Milky Way. Surveys such as *Euclid*,
Rubin and Roman should find thousands (at least <Var name="cosmology/n_euclid_min" /> in *Euclid*
alone), enough to separate CDM from SIDM at up to 5σ.
